01

Polymer – Basics

  • A polymer is a high-molecular-weight macromolecule formed by repeated joining of small molecules called monomers.
  • Monomer → Polymer; n represents the number of repeating units.
  • Example: Ethene (CH₂=CH₂) forms polyethylene (polyethene).
02

Addition Polymerization

  • Also called chain-growth polymerization.
  • Unsaturated monomers join together; no small molecule is eliminated.
  • Driving force: weak C–C π-bonds convert into stronger C–C σ-bonds.
  • Initiators form free radicals, cations or anions that start chain growth.
  • 1,2-addition: π-bond converts to σ-bond while monomer units join.
  • 1,4-addition: monomers with two π-bonds form a new σ-bond and retain a new π-bond; example: butadiene → polybutadiene.
03

Condensation & Copolymerization

  • Condensation polymerization is also called step-growth polymerization.
  • Monomers link together with elimination of small molecules such as H₂O, NH₃ or HCl.
  • Example: terephthalic acid + ethylene glycol → PET.
  • Copolymerization: polymerization of two or more different monomer species.
  • Example given in the notes: Kevlar.
04

Kevlar

  • High-performance aramid fibre in the aromatic polyamide family.
  • Prepared by condensation polymerization; HCl is the by-product.
  • Woven Kevlar fibre is described in the notes as five times stronger than steel.
  • High strength comes from inter-chain hydrogen bonding and aromatic stacking interactions.
  • Rigid molecules form mostly planar, sheet-like structures.
  • Strength decreases at higher temperatures over time.
05

Conducting Polymers

  • Organic polymers that conduct electricity due to conjugated molecular structure and mobile charge carriers.
  • Conjugated π-electron polymers: have conjugated double bonds along the backbone.
  • Overlapping π-orbitals form valence and conduction bands; examples: polyacetylene and polyaniline.
  • P-doping: electrons are removed, creating positive holes; oxidation (e.g. FeCl₃).
  • N-doping: electrons are added, creating negative charges; reduction (e.g. lithium).
06

Extrinsically Conducting Polymers

  • Normally insulating polymers made conducting by adding conductive materials.
  • The polymer does not need a conjugated π-electron backbone.
  • Examples: rubber + carbon black; polyethylene + graphite or carbon black.
  • Conductive element-filled polymers are low-cost, lightweight and easy to process.
  • Applications include computer keyboards.
  • Blended conducting polymers are made by physically mixing an insulating polymer with a conducting polymer or component.
07

Polyaniline (PANI)

  • A conducting polymer with environmental stability; conductivity depends on oxidation state and acid doping.
  • Chemical method: aniline in dilute HCl is treated with ammonium persulphate; precipitate is filtered, washed and dried.
  • Electrochemical method: platinum electrodes are placed in acidified aniline solution and 0.7 V is applied; a thin film forms.
  • Colour changes can be used in sensors and electrochromic devices.
  • Doped PANI is a good conductor; its original form is a semiconductor.
08

Biodegradable Polymers

  • Break down into natural substances such as water, carbon dioxide and biomass with microorganisms.
  • Microorganisms include bacteria, fungi and algae.
  • Enzymatic hydrolysis breaks polymers into oligomers, dimers and monomers, then simpler products.
  • Aerobic: in the presence of oxygen; forms CO₂, water and biomass.
  • Anaerobic: without oxygen; forms methane, biomass, water and CO₂.
  • Applications: packaging, agriculture, medicine, textiles and consumer products.
09

Polylactic Acid (PLA)

  • Biodegradable thermoplastic polyester commonly produced from lactic acid.
  • Renewable sources include corn starch, sugarcane and cassava.
  • Preparation: starch extraction → glucose → fermentation to lactic acid → polymerization.
  • Polymerization methods: direct condensation or ring-opening polymerization of lactide.
  • Contains ester (–COO–) linkages that can break by hydrolysis.
  • Properties: transparent, glossy, stiff; glass transition 60–65°C and melting point about 150–160°C.
  • Uses: packaging, biomedical products, 3D-printing filament, agriculture, textiles and compostable products.
10

Shape-Memory Polymers (SMPs)

  • Smart polymers that return from a temporary deformed shape to their permanent shape when triggered by an external stimulus.
  • Stimuli: temperature, chemicals, light and mechanical force.
  • Programming: heat above Tm, then deform the polymer into a temporary shape.
  • Storage: cool while deformed, below Tm, to fix the temporary shape.
  • Recovery: heat again above Tm without a load; the original shape returns.
  • Applications: heat-shrinkable tubes, self-repairing components, sportswear, automobiles, sutures and vascular stents.
11

PTFE (Polytetrafluoroethylene)

  • Linear polymer with no branching; contains strong and stable C–F bonds.
  • Properties: heat resistance, chemical resistance, electrical insulation and low coefficient of friction.
  • Fluorine atoms pack tightly around the carbon backbone in a twisted zig-zag arrangement.
  • This gives stiffness, high crystalline melting point and thermal stability.
  • Melting point stated in the notes: about 327°C.
  • Mechanical strength remains nearly unchanged from −100°C to 300°C.
  • Cannot be processed by melt extrusion.
